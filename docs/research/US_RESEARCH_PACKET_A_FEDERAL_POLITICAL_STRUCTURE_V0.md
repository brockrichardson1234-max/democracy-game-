## 3. Executive summary

The recognizable federal skeleton is not one undifferentiated “government.” The Constitution vests federal legislative power in a bicameral Congress, executive power in a President, and judicial power in a separate federal judiciary. Statutes separately identify executive departments and agencies. State authority remains structurally relevant to elections and federalism rather than being reducible to federal geography (`USR-CLM-0001`–`0008`, `0047`).

Congress is two distinct chambers. The contemporary House has 435 voting Representatives elected for two-year terms from state-apportioned seats, ordinarily one per district; the Senate has two Senators per state, giving 100 seats across 50 states, with six-year terms staggered in three classes. Both chambers decide questions through Member votes and chamber procedure. Parties and leaders strongly affect agendas and behavior, but the legal vote remains attributable to individual Members (`USR-CLM-0009`–`0026`, `0038`).

The minimum general lawmaking route is not “one congressional vote.” A Member introduces a bill; ordinary practice routes it through committee and then chamber-specific floor procedure; each chamber can amend; both must agree to the same text; differences can be resolved by amendment exchange or conference; the enrolled bill is presented; and the President signs, vetoes, or takes no action under Article I's ten-day rule. A veto can be overridden only by two-thirds votes in both chambers. Committees are practically central but not an invariant legal gate for every possible route (`USR-CLM-0027`–`0037`).

Ordinary passage and ending debate are separate. Most House and Senate questions use a simple majority of those voting with a quorum, subject to exceptions. Senate cloture on legislation generally requires three-fifths of Senators duly chosen and sworn, but cloture is not the vote on passage and is not universally invoked. Reconciliation is an optional special budget process, not a default substitute for the normal bicameral route (`USR-CLM-0017`, `0025`, `0039`–`0042`).

The President holds a four-year constitutional office, subject to the Twenty-Second Amendment, and the term changes at noon January 20. The officeholder, the office, executive institutions, and the administration are therefore distinct facts. The Twenty-Fifth Amendment's vacancy succession is also distinct from the ordinary election-to-transfer route (`USR-CLM-0043`–`0048`).

Presidential popular voting is mediated through elector appointment under state law. Each state's electoral allocation equals its congressional delegation; DC has a separate constitutional allocation. The current 538-elector/270-majority figures are a 2020-apportionment data vintage for the 2024 and 2028 elections, not timeless constants. Current state practice is winner-take-all in 48 states and DC, with Maine and Nebraska using congressional-district plus at-large allocations (`USR-CLM-0049`–`0056`).

The result path contains legally distinct artifacts and decisions: unofficial returns, state/local canvass and certification, a certificate of ascertainment appointing electors, elector meetings and certificates of vote, transmission, the congressional joint-session count, and the declaration of persons elected. The President of the Senate has a ministerial role and no unilateral adjudicatory power. An incoming winner does not receive presidential office authority at any earlier result step; the outgoing term continues until noon January 20 and the successor term then begins (`USR-CLM-0060`–`0072`).

## 5. Primary-source findings

### 5.1 Federal institutions and federalism

Article I creates Congress as a Senate and House, Article II creates the presidential office, and Article III creates one Supreme Court while allowing Congress to establish inferior federal courts. The Tenth Amendment and Supremacy Clause establish a divided federal/state legal topology rather than a unitary federal hierarchy over every governmental fact. Article I's Elections Clause gives states initial responsibility for congressional election mechanics, subject to congressional alteration (`USR-CLM-0001`–`0008`; USR-SRC-0001).

Current Title 5 separately enumerates executive departments and defines executive agency to include departments, government corporations, and independent establishments. The evidence supports institutional separation between President, office, department, agency, and administering official; it does not research the exact Housing institution, which belongs to Packet B (`USR-CLM-0004`, `0047`; USR-SRC-0019).

### 5.2 House and Senate

The House's constitutional term is two years. Current law and official House material support 435 voting Representatives, state apportionment, and single-member districts in states with more than one seat. House vacancies require elections, while Senate vacancies may include temporary executive appointments only when state law authorizes them. The House chooses its Speaker. A majority of either chamber is a constitutional quorum (`USR-CLM-0009`–`0018`, `0026`; USR-SRC-0001–0006).

The Seventeenth Amendment fixes two Senators per state, direct popular election, six-year terms, and one vote per Senator. With 50 states, the contemporary total is 100. The seats remain divided into three classes so approximately one-third face election every two years. The Vice President votes in the Senate only to break a tie (`USR-CLM-0019`–`0025`; USR-SRC-0001, `0009`, `0010`, `0028`).

### 5.3 Enactment

Article I requires both chambers to pass the same bill before presentment. Current House/Senate rules and CRS procedure syntheses support Member introduction, referral, committee and floor action, amendments, and two formal families for resolving different texts: amendments between the houses and conference. The contemporary decline of formal conferences is ordinary-practice evidence, not a change to bicameralism (`USR-CLM-0027`–`0033`; USR-SRC-0005, `0008`, `0011`, `0012`).

Presentment produces legally distinct presidential options. Signature enacts; return with objections triggers the veto route; non-return for ten days excluding Sundays enacts unless adjournment prevents return; and two-thirds votes in both chambers can override. These thresholds and records attach to the relevant procedure, not to party totals (`USR-CLM-0033`, `0036`–`0038`; USR-SRC-0001, `0018`).

### 5.4 Presidential election, count, and transfer

Article II and the Twelfth Amendment make electors separate participants between state appointment and federal office selection. State electoral allocation follows congressional representation, with DC treated as a state only for specified Electoral College purposes. The current 538/270 values are tied to the 2020 apportionment and the 2024/2028 election cycle (`USR-CLM-0049`–`0058`; USR-SRC-0001, `0021`, `0024`, `0026`).

Post-election records must not be collapsed. Current positive-law Title 3 distinguishes the state executive's certificate of ascertainment, the electors' certificate of vote, their transmission, and Congress's count. Section 15 expressly limits the President of the Senate to ministerial duties, limits objections to two grounds with one-fifth support in each chamber, requires both chambers to sustain an objection, and makes the post-count announcement a sufficient declaration of the persons elected (`USR-CLM-0060`–`0070`; USR-SRC-0021, `0023`, `0025`, `0027`).

The Twentieth Amendment and 3 U.S.C. §101 provide the ordinary transfer boundary: the outgoing President remains President until noon January 20 and the successor's term begins then. That office assignment change does not itself recreate Congress, departments, courts, states, or the legal order (`USR-CLM-0045`, `0071`, `0072`; USR-SRC-0001, `0019`, `0022`).

## 6. Formal-rule versus ordinary-practice distinctions

| Subject | Formal rule | Ordinary practice / operational evidence | Preserved boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Committees | Chamber rules provide referral, committee organization, reports, and calendars. | Most consequential legislation follows committee pathways, but bypass/privileged routes exist. | “Normally central” is not “legally mandatory in every case.” |
| House floor access | House rules define privilege and special rules; the House votes to adopt a special rule. | Majority leadership and Rules Committee usually control what reaches the floor and under what terms. | Agenda control is not ownership of Member votes. |
| Senate floor access | Senate rules permit motions to proceed, debate, amendments, and cloture. | The majority leader normally schedules business, often through unanimous consent and negotiation. | Leadership practice is not an exclusive constitutional power. |
| Senate filibuster/cloture | Rule XXII generally requires three-fifths of Senators duly chosen and sworn for legislative cloture. | Cloture is used when ending extended debate/delay is necessary; many matters proceed by consent or without a cloture vote. | Cloture threshold is not the ordinary passage threshold. |
| Bicameral differences | Both chambers must approve identical text. | Amendment exchange is now common; formal conferences are comparatively infrequent; informal negotiation often precedes either. | Practice may choose a vehicle, but cannot remove bicameral agreement. |
| Parties and leadership | Individual Members cast chamber votes under constitutional/chamber procedure. | Party, caucus, leadership, and whip activity can strongly influence choices. | Influence is not canonical ownership of a legislator's vote. |
| Presidential popular result | State law governs canvass/certification and elector appointment within federal constraints. | All states currently use popular results; allocation methods are mostly winner-take-all. | Current practice is mutable and does not directly assign presidential office. |
| Election “certification” | Title 3 names certificates of ascertainment and vote and prescribes the congressional count/declaration. | Public explanations often use “certification” broadly for several stages. | Each artifact and authoritative act retains its own meaning. |

No ordinary-practice claim is used to override controlling constitutional or statutory text.

## 7. Contradictions, ambiguity, and supported bounds

No genuine contradiction among controlling authorities remains unresolved. The following source or scope differences are retained:

1. **House rules page staleness.** The House's general explainer carried a stale sentence about rule adoption. Current-rule claims rely on HMAN-119, whose rules were adopted for the 119th Congress on January 3, 2025. The stale sentence is excluded from support.
2. **Senate class examples.** The Senate classes page had stale example expiration years, while its structural three-class explanation agrees with constitutional text. Packet A relies on the Constitution for the rule and uses the page only as corroborating explanation.
3. **“Proportional” allocation wording.** NARA sometimes labels Maine and Nebraska “proportional.” The actual evidence describes two statewide electors plus one per congressional district; Packet A preserves that structure and does not infer mathematically proportional allocation.
4. **Committee necessity.** Committees are both formal institutions and ordinary practical gates, but not universal legal prerequisites to every possible floor route. This is `SUPPORTED_WITH_BOUNDS`, not a forced yes/no simplification.
5. **Filibuster relevance.** Extended debate can make cloture necessary, but the Senate has no universal rule requiring cloture before passage. The evidence cannot support “every ordinary bill needs 60 votes.”
6. **State certification variation.** The federal interface is uniform enough to identify ascertainment, elector voting, and federal count; the underlying state canvass/certification officers, sequence, and timing vary. Packet A does not generalize one state's path nationally.
7. **Elector behavior.** State appointment and enforcement law is mutable and nonuniform. Packet A establishes the constitutional actor/process distinction but does not establish a single national behavioral rule for electors.
8. **Continuity at transfer.** The presidential term boundary itself does not repeal laws or abolish institutions. A specific program can nonetheless expire, sunset, or lose funding under its own authority; Packet B must retain such facts.
9. **Mutable Code currency.** Preliminary U.S. Code pages state the dates through which laws were incorporated, which ranged from July 7 to August 12, 2026 on the retrieved pages. These version dates are retained, and all mutable P0 dependencies remain subject to mandatory freeze-time revalidation.
