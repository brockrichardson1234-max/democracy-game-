# 2. Accepted central answer

Political pressure and issue formation are accepted conceptually as:

> **A plural, context-specific political process in which real conditions, evidence, frames, recipient cognition, autonomous actors and organizations, directed demands, institutional venues, electoral incentives, and time-bounded governing opportunities produce different issue agendas for different people and institutions. Political issues are provenance-bearing projections over that state—not canonical world objects, universal importance scores, or a hidden national agenda.**

The accepted broad relationship is:

```text
material/social/legal/institutional/external state
→ lived exposure, operational consequence, record,
  measurement, investigation, or evidence
→ bounded actor, organization, outlet,
  institution, or population receipt
→ interpretation through role, values, beliefs,
  objectives, expectations, relationships,
  constituency, and uncertainty
→ problem frame, attribution, demand, priority,
  agenda adoption, organization activity,
  proceeding, communication, or deliberate non-action
→ fragmented media, public, party, legislative,
  state, agency, judicial, campaign, or White House agendas
→ changing recipient belief, attribution, salience,
  willingness, political action, and institutional opportunity
→ valid administration receipt and assessment
→ possible presidential workstream, response,
  monitoring, negotiation, or deliberate inaction
```

No arrow guarantees the next.

# 3. Political issues remain projections

A political-issue projection may group, for one declared producer, consumer, context, and time:

- underlying conditions and occurrences;
- evidence, measurements, and claims;
- problem frames and responsibility attributions;
- affected populations and geographies;
- active actors and organizations;
- political demands and pressure attempts;
- institutional venues and proceedings;
- policy alternatives and governing routes;
- public belief, attribution, importance, and salience estimates;
- electoral and temporal context;
- uncertainty and historical lineage.

The projection owns only its own:

- identity;
- producer/context;
- grouping rationale;
- source references;
- label and version;
- omissions and uncertainty;
- lineage and projection metadata.

It does not own or directly mutate:

- material or social state;
- evidence;
- recipient cognition;
- actor objectives;
- organization agendas;
- media coverage;
- political pressure;
- laws, proposals, schedules, proceedings, or venues;
- White House workstreams;
- ballots or election results.

There is no canonical `Issue: Housing`, `IssueImportance`, `NationalAgenda`, or Top Five Issues state controlling every actor.

# 4. Issue labels and cross-context identity

The same label may refer to different political meanings.

`Housing crisis` might mean:

- renter cost burden;
- home-purchase affordability;
- homelessness;
- construction supply;
- land-use rules;
- mortgage credit;
- disaster recovery;
- federal implementation integrity;
- neighborhood displacement.

Different labels may also overlap in their underlying referents.

`Industrial decline`, `automation displacement`, `trade shock`, `regional unemployment`, and `cost-of-living crisis` may share some evidence or affected populations while retaining different frames, demands, and venues.

Cross-context comparisons must preserve:

- producer/context;
- proposition and frame meaning;
- included/excluded referents;
- population/geography;
- time;
- agenda/pressure/coverage basis;
- support and uncertainty;
- known overlap and lineage.

Labels alone do not establish identity, independence, equivalence, contradiction, or additivity.

# 5. Recipient cognition remains independent of issue projections

Recipient-owned:

- belief;
- uncertainty;
- attribution;
- attitude importance;
- cognitive accessibility;
- salience;
- preference;
- trust;
- memory;
- willingness;
- evaluation

must attach to independently meaningful semantic targets rather than requiring a political-issue projection ID.

Permitted targets may include:

- propositions;
- problem frames;
- conditions or occurrences;
- actors, institutions, parties, candidates, or organizations;
- policy alternatives;
- responsibility, credit, or competence relationships;
- historical events and promises;
- deliberately broad concern families whose scope and lineage are explicit.

An issue projection may summarize that cognition. It does not create it.

## 5.1 Broad and narrow cognition

A person may genuinely consider `Housing affordability` important without possessing separate exact attitudes about zoning, mortgage rates, homelessness, construction subsidies, or federal implementation.

Broad concern does not automatically populate narrow concern.

Narrow states remain:

- independently supported;
- modeled;
- bounded;
- unsupported;
- or unknown.

## 5.2 Split

Splitting a projection creates new views over retained targets.

It does not:

- divide broad cognition by fiat;
- copy one concern into every child;
- invent child-level belief;
- duplicate pressure or affected population.

## 5.3 Merge or bundle

Merging projections or bundling issues for legislation, campaigns, or communication does not merge recipient state.

A person may support one component and oppose another.

## 5.4 Reframing

A new frame receives identity/version and lineage.

It does not overwrite earlier frames, beliefs, claims, or historical meaning.

# 6. Issue-projection production and surfacing

Every persisted or load-bearing issue projection identifies:

- producer or analytical owner;
- intended consumer;
- access class;
- creation/as-of time;
- accessible source set;
- selection/grouping method;
- label source;
- included and excluded semantic targets;
- population/geography/time support;
- uncertainty and blind spots;
- lineage and recompute/retention semantics;
- whether it is persisted evidence, ephemeral query, or presentation navigation.

A projection may be produced for:

- White House analysis;
- congressional research;
- party or campaign strategy;
- organization planning;
- media analysis;
- public-agenda measurement;
- State-of-the-Nation navigation;
- historical analysis;
- player-requested review;
- developer/audit use kept separate from live play.

A hidden projector may not scan canonical truth, choose the most dramatic clusters, and thereby cause actors or the player to treat them as important.

Creating or displaying a projection does not cause:

- agenda adoption;
- coverage;
- salience;
- organization;
- pressure;
- venue admission;
- presidential attention.

Actors may act without a common issue projection existing.

# 7. Political relevance dimensions remain separate

The accepted design distinguishes:

- material severity;
- prevalence;
- concentration;
- direction/trend;
- direct experience;
- evidence strength;
- observability;
- recipient salience;
- attitude importance;
- cognitive accessibility;
- emotional intensity;
- actor priority;
- organization mobilization;
- media attention;
- directed political pressure;
- urgency;
- feasibility;
- institutional agenda state;
- decision readiness;
- electoral consequence;
- presidential attention.

No one dimension silently substitutes for another.

Examples:

- a condition can be severe but poorly observed;
- an issue can be salient but lack a viable route;
- a procedural deadline can be urgent but publicly obscure;
- a media controversy can be intense but materially small;
- an attitude can be important but not recently accessible;
- an issue can affect an election differently across geographies and coalitions;
- a presidential decision can be ready before the public cares.

# 8. Plural agenda authority

There is no single canonical national agenda.

The following remain separate:

## 8.1 Adopted priority

Actor-, office-, organization-, party-, campaign-, or institution-owned state created by a valid decision or governance process.

## 8.2 Scheduled/procedural agenda item

Institution/procedure-owned state such as a hearing, floor item, argument, rulemaking stage, budget deadline, board vote, or convention item.

## 8.3 Allocated resources or attention

Owner-specific operational state such as an assigned reporter, field staff, advertising budget, legislative negotiator, or presidential calendar block.

## 8.4 Recipient salience or importance

Recipient-owned cognition.

## 8.5 Analytical agenda projection

A read-only measurement or summary over actual state.

Examples include:

- public agenda estimate;
- media coverage agenda;
- congressional agenda view;
- party or campaign emphasis view;
- state agenda view;
- White House portfolio view.

An analytical projection cannot satisfy a process requiring actual adoption, scheduling, allocation, vote, or action.

A party's perceived association with an issue does not prove that it formally adopted the issue or a particular solution.

# 9. Agenda adoption, revision, and non-adoption

A consequential agenda entry requires an owner-specific process:

```text
bounded information, experience, duty,
relationship, or opportunity
→ owner interpretation
→ available agenda decision
→ priority, schedule, resource allocation,
  position, workstream, investigation,
  monitoring, or deliberate non-adoption
→ owner-held state or occurrence
```

An entry may be:

- elevated;
- narrowed;
- broadened;
- reframed;
- delegated;
- scheduled;
- displaced;
- withdrawn;
- fulfilled;
- defeated;
- superseded;
- expired;
- retained for monitoring.

An actor may know and care but decline adoption because no viable venue exists, evidence is weak, resources are committed elsewhere, action is politically risky, another owner has the duty, the organization is divided, or delay is strategic.

The issue projection cannot write agenda state.

# 10. Problem recognition and framing

A canonical condition is not automatically a political problem.

A problem frame may connect:

- observed or believed condition;
- expectation, baseline, right, duty, promise, value, or risk standard;
- affected population, place, institution, or interest;
- asserted cause or responsibility;
- urgency and time horizon;
- desired response, venue, or solution family;
- evidence, uncertainty, and omitted alternatives.

Frames are actor-, organization-, institution-, media-, or population-context interpretations.

They may be:

- accurate;
- mistaken;
- misleading;
- exaggerated;
- incomplete;
- contested;
- normative;
- strategically framed.

A false or weakly supported frame may generate real belief, mobilization, hearings, litigation, campaign activity, or presidential pressure.

Those political consequences do not make the frame true.

# 11. Political-pressure contract

Political pressure is not one universal number.

It arises through actual directed attempts, observed constraints, relationships, resources, institutions, and target interpretation.

## 11.1 Closed stages

The accepted stages are:

```text
source intention
→ pressure attempt
→ delivery/public availability
→ target receipt
→ target interpretation
→ actual constraint or consequence
→ target response
```

These facts remain distinct.

## 11.2 Pressure attempts

Potential attempts include:

- constituent contacts;
- requests, offers, threats, or commitments;
- legislative bargaining and vote conditions;
- organization lobbying;
- endorsements and opposition;
- advertising;
- fundraising or contribution decisions;
- primary recruitment or challenge;
- protest, petition, boycott, or strike;
- lawsuit or regulatory petition;
- oversight, hearing, subpoena, or investigation;
- state/intergovernmental demand;
- media inquiry or publication;
- public campaign;
- internal dissent or resignation threat.

Every load-bearing attempt identifies source, target/audience, demand or proposition, route, resources, population/geography, time, access, evidence, relationship context, and independent resolver.

## 11.3 Consequential occurrences without pressure intent

A firm closure, court ruling, disaster, market movement, agency failure, or material condition may constrain political choices without having been intended as persuasion.

The original occurrence must be labeled accurately.

Political pressure may arise later through:

- target interpretation;
- third-party communication;
- organization action;
- media/public reaction;
- institutional consequence;
- directed demand.

The design may not invent influence intent merely because an occurrence creates pressure.

## 11.4 Receiver sovereignty

The target owns whether an attempt is received, credible, costly, persuasive, threatening, useful, ignored, publicized, resisted, or answered.

No source reputation or pressure summary guarantees compliance.

## 11.5 Duplicate-pressure protection

The same action, participants, demand, evidence, organization resources, or occurrence may not count as several independent pressure sources merely because several issue projections, media artifacts, workstreams, or dashboards reference it.

A coalition statement signed by ten organizations is one statement occurrence with ten signatories. The signatories may matter, but the statement does not become ten independent publications.

Distinct target-specific contacts remain distinct when they actually occurred.

---

# 12. Organization and mobilization

Material harm does not automatically create an organization or organized constituency.

Organization and collective action require accepted combinations of:

- preexisting networks or institutions;
- leadership;
- membership/constituency relationships;
- communication and recruitment;
- resources;
- governance;
- coordination;
- legal/practical opportunity;
- perceived benefit and risk;
- actual action.

Affected people may remain diffuse, divided, unaware, distrustful, unable to coordinate, or focused on other concerns.

Willingness to act is not attendance, donation, strike, protest, turnout, or vote.

Aggregate participation processes produce distributions, counts, flows, or bounded actions rather than one public hive mind.

---

# 13. Institutional venues and venue choice

A venue is an institution, procedure, forum, or route capable of receiving a particular kind of demand or action.

Potential venues include:

- congressional committees and chambers;
- appropriations and budget vehicles;
- executive offices and agencies;
- rulemaking and enforcement;
- courts;
- state and local governments;
- parties and campaigns;
- elections;
- organization governance;
- media/public communication;
- bargaining, market, or labor processes;
- external/diplomatic institutions where supported.

The venue owns:

- standing;
- admission;
- authority;
- procedure;
- calendar or queue;
- resolution.

Actors may choose, switch, add, or avoid venues based on their frame, desired remedy, authority, evidence, relationships, resources, timing, risk, and prior outcomes.

Venue shopping is an actor action.

It does not guarantee admission or success.

The same broad issue may proceed in several venues at once with different timelines and outcomes.

---

# 14. Political opportunity and governing windows

A political or governing opportunity is a projection over actual:

- problem recognition;
- available policy or response alternative;
- venue and procedure;
- actor/coalition state;
- authority;
- fiscal and administrative feasibility;
- resources;
- evidence;
- deadlines;
- public/media context;
- electoral timing;
- willingness to act.

No hidden event declares a policy window open because the game needs a decision.

A solution may predate the recognized problem.

A problem may have no viable solution.

A window may open or close because of real committee schedules, must-pass legislation, court rulings, budget cycles, vacancies, measurements, organization activity, coalition changes, state action, crises, elections, expiring authority, or resource changes.

An available window does not guarantee success.

---

# 15. Politics cannot manufacture authority

Political salience, urgency, nationalization, public support, media attention, pressure, or agenda adoption cannot by itself create:

- legal authority;
- fiscal authority;
- standing;
- jurisdiction;
- eligibility;
- procedural admission;
- operational capability;
- an unavailable action route.

Thus:

```text
politically urgent
≠ legal emergency
```

```text
broad public support
≠ budget authority
```

```text
nationally discussed
≠ federal jurisdiction
```

```text
prominent issue
≠ court or committee admission
```

Politics can motivate attempts to obtain or use authority. The relevant institutions resolve those attempts.

---

# 16. Competition, coexistence, reinforcement, and displacement

There is no universal maximum number of political issues.

Owner-specific constraints may exist in:

- recipient attention and memory;
- outlet space and investigative capacity;
- party/campaign messaging;
- legislative calendars;
- organization staff and money;
- White House assignments and presidential calendar;
- agency queues;
- legal venue capacity.

Issues may compete for those resources.

They may also coexist because they involve different people, places, institutions, venues, or resources.

They may reinforce one another through represented shared causes, affected populations, blame, identity, organization, legislation, or framing.

Displacement occurs only when a real owner reallocates attention, schedule, staff, money, memory, or opportunity.

Displacement in one agenda does not erase the issue elsewhere or change the underlying condition.

# 17. Issue dynamics

The following describe possible contextual projection relationships, not one universal issue state machine:

## 17.1 Emergence

Enough separately owned facts exist for a declared actor, organization, institution, analytical producer, or interface to form a meaningful political projection or adopt a priority.

Emergence need not be national.

## 17.2 Intensification

An issue may intensify through different combinations of worsening conditions, broader/concentrated exposure, stronger evidence, organization growth, consequential actor action, venue admission, coverage, salience, deadline, electoral concentration, or prior policy failure.

These dimensions need not move together.

## 17.3 Regionalization

An issue may remain regionally bounded through geography, affected population, evidence, organization, media, institutions, and venue access.

Regional does not mean minor.

## 17.4 Nationalization

Nationalization requires actual bridges such as national evidence, federal action, national media coverage, party/campaign adoption, multistate coordination, national organization activity, presidential communication, or cross-regional effects.

One headline does not nationalize an issue for every recipient or institution.

Nationalization does not create federal authority.

## 17.5 Splitting

A broad projection may split when frames, affected groups, evidence, causes, solutions, or venues diverge.

Split children preserve lineage and do not duplicate underlying facts, recipient cognition, population, or pressure.

## 17.6 Merging and bundling

Actors may bundle separate matters for legislation, coalition building, communication, or campaigns.

The bundle has an actor/process and purpose.

It does not merge underlying domains, proceedings, or recipient state.

## 17.7 Reframing

A new frame changes language, cause, responsibility, affected-group emphasis, values, urgency, or venue while preserving historical lineage.

## 17.8 Fading and dormancy

An issue may fade in one context while the underlying condition, law, organization, memory, or obligation remains.

## 17.9 Resurgence

A dormant issue may return through new condition change, evidence, implementation failure, investigation, court action, actor revival, election, crisis, or reinterpretation.

Resurgence requires traceable new and retained state rather than a random story trigger.

# 18. Partisanship, factions, and issue ownership

Modern party identity may strongly shape trust, attribution, frame adoption, candidate evaluation, organization response, and willingness to mobilize.

It does not create one party-wide issue map.

Party leadership, legislative caucuses, factions, governors, campaigns, activists, organizations, and voters may prioritize different matters.

Issue ownership is an audience perception, not objective ownership of a domain.

It may concern:

- who appears to care most;
- who is most strongly associated with the issue;
- who is perceived as competent;
- who has the preferred position;
- who bears responsibility for current conditions.

Those dimensions may disagree.

A party may be associated with an issue yet distrusted to solve it. A voter may perceive competence while opposing the party's policy.

Reputation may change through emphasis, performance, implementation, visible responsibility, candidate record, evidence, scandal, and long-term memory. No message guarantees ownership transfer.

---

# 19. Electoral translation

Public salience, importance, concern, agreement, blame, competence evaluation, turnout willingness, and ballot choice remain separate.

The broad chain is:

```text
condition or political occurrence
→ evidence and communication
→ recipient notice, belief, attribution, and salience
→ evaluation of parties, candidates, and institutions
→ campaign and organization activity
→ turnout and vote willingness
→ election procedure
→ ballots, count, result, certification,
  entitlement, and office assignment
```

A recipient may care deeply while:

- perceiving little candidate difference;
- supporting one policy but trusting another actor's competence;
- blaming a governor, Congress, firms, or prior administration;
- believing no actor can solve the problem;
- preferring inaction;
- voting on another issue;
- not turning out.

Electoral concentration depends on valid population, geography, belief, attribution, and contest state.

No issue projection directly modifies election results.

---

# 20. Policy feedback

Policies, programs, taxes, benefits, regulations, enforcement, institutions, and implementation may reshape later politics through:

- material experience;
- eligibility and participation;
- administrative burden;
- provider and organization relationships;
- visible or hidden government role;
- funding dependencies;
- state variation;
- legal rights and obligations;
- evidence and measurement;
- public claims and attribution;
- constituency formation;
- actor resources and incentives.

Feedback is not guaranteed.

A beneficial policy may remain politically invisible, poorly attributed, unevenly implemented, distrusted, weakly organized, or displaced by another issue.

A harmful policy may also fail to generate opposition when evidence, organization, attribution, or access is weak.

Policy feedback can alter later populations, organizations, relationships, agendas, venues, feasibility, and governing opportunities through their proper owners.

# 21. Presidential agenda setting

The President may, within valid authority and information:

- create or elevate a White House workstream;
- request analysis or monitoring;
- emphasize a frame or proposition;
- release evidence;
- propose legislation or budget action;
- direct or delegate administration work;
- negotiate with actors and organizations;
- appoint personnel;
- use legal or administrative routes;
- support, oppose, or negotiate another actor's initiative;
- bundle or reframe administration objectives;
- tolerate divergence;
- ignore, delay, or deprioritize a matter.

The President cannot directly:

- set public salience;
- set a media, party, congressional, state, or organization agenda;
- make an issue national;
- create political pressure;
- force a policy window;
- make a frame true;
- assign electoral consequences;
- delete a controversy with a statement.

Agenda-setting attempts may fail after consuming real calendar, staff, bargaining, fiscal, legal, and communication opportunities.

That failure is valid gameplay.

# 22. Presidential attention remains downstream

A condition, issue projection, media trend, public-priority estimate, organization campaign, or institutional agenda reaches the President only through:

```text
valid artifact, communication, or record
→ administration receipt
→ office interpretation and coordination
→ accepted escalation basis
→ presidential review or decision
```

No political-issue projection may directly stop time or create a decision.

A White House workstream is separately adopted administration state.

Monitoring or Country Watch visibility does not automatically create a workstream.

# 24. Historical persistence and generated prehistory

History may preserve load-bearing:

- conditions and evidence;
- problem frames;
- recipient cognition where retained;
- agenda adoption/removal;
- pressure attempts and consequences;
- organization and venue action;
- issue-projection versions and lineage;
- policy proposals and outcomes;
- workstream decisions;
- what actors knew at decision time;
- later reinterpretations.

History does not own current salience or agenda state.

Lower-resolution prehistory need preserve issue detail only when later play consumes it, such as:

- durable party/faction association;
- campaign promise;
- major failed reform;
- active organization;
- visible policy feedback;
- unresolved implementation conflict;
- court/state/federal venue history;
- persistent blame or competence reputation;
- inherited agenda/workstream state.

A current issue cannot retroactively manufacture that actors cared earlier.

---

# 25. Time, feedback, and deterministic continuation

Issue-related processes may operate on different cadences.

There is no universal daily `updateIssues()` turn.

Causally dependent same-time events require declared semantic order.

Independent events must not vary by handler or container order.

Feedback cycles among organization, media, recipients, parties, institutions, and opportunities require explicit timing and bounded continuation/saturation semantics.

Stochastic agenda, mobilization, coverage, salience, pressure, and venue processes must use stable causal inputs and identities and remain save/load and time-chunk invariant.

No global random cursor decides which issue becomes important.

# 26. Accepted adversarial proof set

At contract level, the accepted composite supports:

1. severe regional employment deterioration remaining politically quiet;
2. a materially small symbolic controversy becoming politically large without changing its material scale;
3. one Housing condition sustaining several overlapping but nonidentical issue maps;
4. energy problems splitting and later being bundled in legislation without merging domains or cognition;
5. autonomous Congress creating a proposal and pressure before White House adoption;
6. a presidential agenda-setting attempt failing despite resource expenditure;
7. the same beneficial policy producing strong or weak political feedback depending on visibility, attribution, organization, and competing attention;
8. a false causal frame producing real political action without changing underlying truth;
9. a security incident displacing Housing in some agendas while Housing remains active in others;
10. a broad concern surviving issue-projection split/merge without being copied or erased.

These are paper contract proofs, not generated simulation output.
